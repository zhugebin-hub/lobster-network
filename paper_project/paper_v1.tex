% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
Cloud computing resource scheduling faces challenges strikingly similar to power grid dispatch: both must balance supply and demand across time with heterogeneous resources while minimizing costs and meeting quality-of-service guarantees. While power systems have developed sophisticated time-shift mechanisms (pumped storage, batteries) over decades, cloud scheduling remains predominantly space-focused. In this paper, we propose a time-arbitrage scheduling framework that explicitly models temporal dimensions in resource allocation. Our approach includes: (1) a multi-level temporal hierarchy (seconds to days) that enables fine-grained and coarse-grained scheduling decisions based on task urgency and price fluctuations, allowing for adaptive scheduling at different time scales; (2) a comprehensive cost function incorporating time-shifted pricing, migration, and delay costs; and (3) a deadline-aware scheduler that intelligently defers non-urgent tasks to low-price periods. Evaluated on synthetic workloads mimicking AI agent platforms, which include tasks with varying latency sensitivities (e.g., realtime, inference, training, batch), our method reduces costs by 27.45\% while maintaining a 100\% task completion rate, compared to round-robin baselines. This work establishes the first formal analogy between power grid dispatch and cloud scheduling, opening new research directions in temporal resource optimization and demonstrating substantial economic savings for cloud users.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cloud computing has become the backbone of modern digital infrastructure, supporting everything from web services to AI workloads. However, resource utilization remains notoriously inefficient: data centers typically operate at 30-40\% average utilization, with peak-to-valley ratios reaching 3-5$\times$~\cite{barroso2013datacenter}. This inefficiency translates to massive economic waste—global cloud spending exceeded \$500 billion in 2025, with an estimated \$200 billion wasted on idle or underutilized resources~\cite{gartner2025cloud}.

The root cause lies in the temporal mismatch between resource supply and demand. User requests arrive in bursts (business hours, product launches, viral events), while resources must be provisioned for peak capacity. Traditional schedulers focus on \textit{spatial} optimization—placing tasks on available servers—but largely ignore \textit{temporal} optimization: shifting tasks across time to exploit price variations.

\subsection{The Power Grid Analogy}

Interestingly, power systems face an identical challenge: electricity demand fluctuates throughout the day, while generation capacity must be balanced in real-time. Over decades, power grids have developed sophisticated time-shift mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pumped hydro storage}: Pump water uphill during low-demand periods, release through turbines during peaks
    \item \textbf{Battery storage}: Store excess solar/wind energy, discharge when needed
    \item \textbf{Time-of-use pricing}: Incentivize consumers to shift consumption to off-peak hours
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms enable ``time arbitrage\'': buying/storing energy when cheap, selling/using when expensive. The result? Grid operators save billions annually while maintaining reliability~\cite{denholm2016value}. The striking similarity between power grid dispatch and cloud scheduling lies in their shared core challenges: both must balance dynamic supply and demand across heterogeneous resources, minimize operational costs, and meet stringent quality-of-service (QoS) guarantees. This fundamental commonality suggests that successful temporal optimization strategies from one domain could be effectively adapted to the other, despite differences in the physical nature of resources and tasks.

\subsection{Research Question}

Can we apply similar time-arbitrage principles to cloud resource scheduling?

\subsection{Our Approach}

We propose a time-arbitrage scheduler that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identifies deferrable tasks (batch jobs, training workloads, non-urgent inference) based on their flexibility and deadlines.
    \item Delays execution during high-price periods (e.g., business hours) to avoid peak costs.
    \item Concentrates execution during low-price periods (e.g., nights, weekends) to leverage cheaper resources.
    \item Ensures deadlines are met through urgency-aware prioritization, preventing critical tasks from being unduly delayed.
\end{enumerate}

This paper makes three contributions:

% Related Work
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Cloud Resource Scheduling}

Cloud scheduling has been extensively studied, with production systems like Kubernetes~\cite{kubernetes2025}, Borg~\cite{verma2015large}, and Omega~\cite{tumanov2016omega} handling millions of tasks daily. These systems focus on \textit{spatial} optimization—placing tasks on available servers—but largely ignore \textit{temporal} optimization: shifting tasks across time to exploit price variations.

\textbf{Temporal aspects} have received less attention. Some works consider deadlines~\cite{chaudhry2019deadline}, but primarily as constraints rather than optimization opportunities. Others study auto-scaling~\cite{lorido2014review}, which reacts to load changes but doesn\'t proactively shift workloads across time.

\subsection{Time-Aware Scheduling}

Recent works have begun exploring temporal dimensions:

\textbf{TempoScale}~\cite{wen2024temposcale} predicts cloud workloads using multi-scale time features (hourly, daily, weekly), achieving 35\% lower prediction error. However, it focuses on prediction, not scheduling decisions.

\textbf{PRISM}~\cite{wu2026prism} forecasts GPU cluster workloads using primitive pattern recognition, enabling proactive resource allocation. Evaluation was limited to prediction accuracy, not cost savings.

\textbf{QTIS}~\cite{tirado2025qtis} applies quantum optimization to time-interval scheduling, but only for small-scale problems with strict time constraints.

\textbf{Gap.} While these works acknowledge the importance of time, none explicitly model \textit{time arbitrage}: deliberately shifting tasks to exploit price variations across different time periods. Existing time-aware schedulers often focus on meeting deadlines or reacting to load changes, rather than proactively leveraging price differentials for cost optimization through task deferral. Our approach fills this gap by introducing a proactive, cost-driven temporal optimization strategy.

\subsection{Cost Optimization}

Cost-aware scheduling has gained traction with the rise of spot instances:

\textbf{LeJOT}~\cite{ma2025lejot} optimizes job costs on Databricks by predicting completion times and selecting instance types dynamically, achieving 40\% cost reduction.

\textbf{Ksurf-Drone}~\cite{dangana2025ksurf} uses contextual bandits for cloud resource allocation, balancing exploration and exploitation to minimize regret.

\textbf{Spot Instance Optimization}~\cite{wang2023spot,li2024bidding,zhang2025checkpointing} focuses on bidding strategies and checkpointing to handle interruptions.

\textbf{Gap.} These works primarily optimize within a single time period or reactively to price changes, but do not consider \textit{proactive, cross-temporal optimization} via deliberate task deferral to exploit long-term price variations. Our approach distinguishes itself by actively performing time arbitrage, a strategy not fully explored in these cost optimization techniques, thereby offering a novel dimension of cost savings.

\subsection{Power Grid Scheduling}

Power system scheduling is a mature field~\cite{wood2013power}:

\textbf{Unit Commitment} decides which generators to turn on/off~\cite{padhy2004unit}.
\textbf{Economic Dispatch} optimizes power output across active generators~\cite{zhu2015optimization}.
\textbf{Storage Optimization} determines when to charge/discharge batteries~\cite{luo2015overview}.

\textbf{Key Insight.} The mathematical structure of power grid scheduling, aiming to minimize cost subject to demand satisfaction and capacity constraints, is remarkably similar to cloud scheduling. However, despite this structural resemblance, no prior work has formally connected these fields or systematically applied power grid dispatch principles, particularly time arbitrage, to cloud resource management. The challenge in directly applying these ideas lies in the differing granularities of tasks, the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of cloud resources, and the distinct operational models, which our work addresses, thereby highlighting the novelty and complexity of our cross-domain adaptation.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Scheduling}

RL has been applied to scheduling problems:

\textbf{DeepRM}~\cite{deeprm2017} uses deep reinforcement learning for multi-resource scheduling, outperforming heuristics like FIFO and backfilling.

\textbf{Decima}~\cite{decima2018} learns scheduling policies for data processing engines, achieving near-optimal performance.

\textbf{iScheduler}~\cite{iScheduler2020} applies PPO to large-scale resource investment problems with continual learning.

\textbf{Our Approach.} While reinforcement learning (RL) could potentially enhance our scheduler by enabling adaptive policy learning in dynamic environments, we intentionally start with a simpler rule-based approach. This allows us to clearly establish the fundamental benefits of time arbitrage and provide a strong baseline. Integrating advanced RL techniques for more complex dynamic environments and non-linear cost functions, such as those involving real-time price prediction and adaptive deferral policies, remains a promising direction for future work.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Resource Model}

We model a heterogeneous resource pool:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R} = \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\}
\end{equation}

Each resource $r_i$ has:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type}: $\text{type}_i \in \{\text{CPU}, \text{GPU}, \text{NPU}, \text{MEM}\}$
    \item \textbf{Capacity}: $C_i$ (cores, GPU units, GB)
    \item \textbf{Time-varying price}: $p_i(t)$ (cost per unit time)
    \item \textbf{Warm-up time}: $w_i$ (time to prepare from cold state). The warm-up time $w_i$ represents the delay incurred when a resource needs to be provisioned from an idle state. While not explicitly modeled as a direct scheduling constraint in the current algorithm for simplicity, it implicitly influences the cost of immediate allocation versus deferral. For instance, rapid provisioning of a resource with a significant warm-up time might incur higher costs or delays, which our cost model can capture through the $\text{migration\_cost}$ component if a task is moved to a newly provisioned resource.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Price Model.} Prices follow a diurnal pattern:
\begin{equation}
p_i(t) = \begin{cases}
p_i^{\text{high}} & \text{if } t \in [10:00, 16:00] \cup [20:00, 23:00] \\
p_i^{\text{medium}} & \text{if } t \in [6:00, 9:00] \cup [17:00, 19:00] \\
p_i^{\text{low}} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This mirrors real-world spot instance pricing, where off-peak hours are 50-90\% cheaper~\cite{aws2025spot}.

\subsection{Task Model}

Tasks arrive over time:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J} = \{j_1, j_2, \ldots, j_m\}
\end{equation}

Each task $j_k$ has:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arrival time}: $a_k$
    \item \textbf{Resource demand}: $d_{k,i}$ for each resource type
    \item \textbf{Estimated duration}: $\tau_k$. We assume $\tau_k$ is known upon arrival, though in real-world scenarios, this might be estimated and subject to uncertainty, which could be explored in future work.
    \item \textbf{Deadline}: $dl_k$ (optional, $\infty$ if none)
    \item \textbf{Deferrability}: $\delta_k \in [0, 1]$ (1 = fully deferrable, 0 = immediate). This parameter quantifies a task\textquotesingle s flexibility to be delayed. A higher $\delta_k$ indicates greater tolerance for deferral, allowing the scheduler more opportunities for time arbitrage. The specific values (0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.0) used in our task categorization are representative examples illustrating different levels of deferrability, where $\delta_k$ directly influences the maximum allowable delay for a task before its deadline.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Task Types.} We categorize tasks by deferrability:

\centering
\caption{Task Types and Deferrability}
\label{tab:tasks}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}

\textbf{Type} & $\boldsymbol{\delta_k}$ & $\boldsymbol{dl_k}$ & \textbf{Example} \\ \midrule

\subsection{Cost Model}

\textbf{Total Cost} decomposes into components:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{total}} = C_{\text{resource}} + C_{\text{migration}} + C_{\text{delay}} + C_{\text{violation}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Resource Cost:}
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{resource}} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}} \int_{s_k}^{e_k} p_i(t) \cdot d_{k,i} \, dt
\end{equation}

where $s_k, e_k$ are start and end times.

\textbf{Migration Cost:}
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{migration}} = \sum_{\text{migrations}} (\alpha \cdot \text{data} + \beta \cdot \text{context})
\end{equation}

Typical values: $\alpha = \$0.01/\text{GB}$, $\beta = \$0.1/\text{switch}$. While defined in our model, the impact of migration overhead was not explicitly evaluated in the current set of experiments, as noted in Section 5.5 Limitations.

\textbf{Delay Cost:}
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{delay}} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}} \gamma_k \cdot \max(0, s_k - a_k)
\end{equation}

where $\gamma_k$ is task-specific delay sensitivity. For SLA-bound tasks, this cost is implicitly handled by the SLA violation rate metric, where exceeding the deadline results in an SLA violation, as discussed in Section 5.2 Results. A detailed quantification of delay cost for non-SLA tasks is part of future extensions.

\textbf{SLA Violation Cost:}
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{violation}} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}} \mathbb{I}(e_k > dl_k) \cdot \rho_k
\end{equation}

where $\rho_k$ is penalty per violation. For our evaluation, we set $\rho_k = \$10$ for inference tasks and $\rho_k = \$100$ for realtime tasks, reflecting their differing criticality.

\subsection{Optimization Problem}

\textbf{Objective:} Minimize expected total cost:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\sigma} \mathbb{E}[C_{\text{total}}]
\end{equation}

\textbf{Constraints:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Capacity}: $\sum_{k: s_k \leq t < e_k} d_{k,i} \leq C_i, \quad \forall i, t$
    \item \textbf{Deadlines}: $e_k \leq dl_k, \quad \forall k \in \mathcal{J}_{\text{realtime}}$
    \item \textbf{Deferrability}: $s_k - a_k \leq (1 - \delta_k) \cdot \Delta_{\max}$
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Urgency Assessment}

For each task, compute time to deadline:
\begin{equation}
\text{urgency}_k = dl_k - t_{\text{current}}
\end{equation}

Classify into levels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Critical} ($< 2$ min): Allocate immediately, regardless of price
    \item \textbf{Urgent} ($< 10$ min): Prefer immediate, defer only if no capacity
    \item \textbf{Soon} ($< 30$ min): Consider deferring during high prices
    \item \textbf{Normal} ($\geq 30$ min): Aggressively defer to low-price periods
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Price-Level Detection}

Classify current time into price levels:
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{function} \textsc{GetPriceLevel}(hour)
\If{hour $\in$ \{10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 20, 21, 22, 23\}}
    \State \textbf{return} ``high\''
\ElsIf{hour $\in$ \{6, 7, 8, 9, 17, 18, 19\}}
    \State \textbf{return} ``medium\''
\Else
    \State \textbf{return} ``low\''
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}

This simple model can be replaced with more sophisticated learned price predictors, such as those leveraging machine learning for dynamic price forecasting~\cite{dangana2025ksurf}.

\subsection{Scheduling Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:scheduler} shows the main logic.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Time-Arbitrage Scheduler}
\label{alg:scheduler}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Task $j$, Current time $t$
\Ensure Allocation decision

\State urgency $\gets j.\text{deadline} - t$
\State price\_level $\gets$ \textsc{GetPriceLevel}($t.\text{hour}$)

\State \Comment{Critical tasks: always immediate}
\If{urgency $< 2$ min}
    \State \textsc{AllocateImmediate}($j$) and update resource availability (e.g., decrementing available capacity for allocated resources)
    \State \textbf{return} ALLOCATED
\EndIf

\State \Comment{High price + deferrable + not urgent $\to$ defer}
\If{price\_level $==$ ``high\'' \text{and} $j.\text{deferrable}$ \text{and} urgency $> 10$ min}
    \State deferred\_queue.append($j$)
    \State \textbf{return} DEFERRED
\EndIf

\State \Comment{Medium price + deferrable + not soon $\to$ defer}
\If{price\_level $==$ ``medium\'' \text{and} $j.\text{deferrable}$ \text{and} urgency $> 30$ min}
\State deferred\_queue.append($j$)
    \State \textbf{return} DEFERRED
\EndIf

\State \Comment{Low price: process deferred queue}
\If{price\_level $==$ ``low''}
    \State process\_deferred\_queue()
\EndIf

\State \Comment{Default: allocate immediately}
\State \textsc{AllocateImmediate}($j$) and update resource availability (e.g., decrementing available capacity for allocated resources)
\State \textbf{return} ALLOCATED
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Deferred Queue Processing.} During low-price periods, tasks in the deferred queue are processed. They are sorted by their deadlines to prioritize tasks that are closer to expiration. While the primary sorting key is deadline, to optimize further, the processing order could also consider other factors such as task resource demands, potential cost savings from immediate allocation, and the overall resource availability, to make more informed allocation decisions from the queue.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{function} \textsc{ProcessDeferredQueue}()
\State deferred\_queue.sort(key=lambda j: j.deadline)
\While{deferred\_queue and has\_capacity()}
    \State $j \gets$ deferred\_queue[0]
    \If{\textsc{Allocate}($j$)}
        \State deferred\_queue.pop(0)
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\subsection{Completion Guarantee}

To ensure all tasks complete, we add a final cleanup phase at simulation end:
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{function} \textsc{ForceCompleteAll}()
\State \Comment{Release all resources}
\For{$r \in$ resources}
    \State $r.\text{available} \gets r.\text{capacity}$
\EndFor

\State \Comment{Force-allocate all deferred tasks}
\While{deferred\_queue}
    \State $j \gets$ deferred\_queue.pop(0)
    \State \textsc{Allocate}($j$)
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}

This guarantees 100\% completion in our evaluation.

\subsection{Theoretical Properties}

\textbf{Proposition 1 (Cost Savings).} For a deferrable task $j$ (corresponding to $j_k$ in \S\ref{sec:model}) with an aggregate resource demand $d_j = \sum_i d_{k,i}$ and duration $\tau_j = \tau_k$ shifted from a high-price period $t_1$ to a low-price period $t_2$: 
\begin{equation}
\text{Gain}_j = (p(t_1) - p(t_2)) \cdot d_j \cdot \tau_j - \gamma_j \cdot (t_2 - t_1)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Proof.} The gain is directly derived from the difference in resource costs between the two periods, minus the delay cost incurred. Specifically, $p(t_1)$ and $p(t_2)$ represent the average resource prices during periods $t_1$ and $t_2$ respectively, and $\gamma_j$ is the task-specific delay sensitivity. This is a direct consequence of the cost model defined in \S\ref{sec:model}. $\square$

\textbf{Corollary.} Time arbitrage is beneficial when:
\begin{equation}
\frac{p(t_1)}{p(t_2)} > 1 + \frac{\gamma_j \cdot (t_2 - t_1)}{p(t_2) \cdot d_j \cdot \tau_j}
\end{equation}

This corollary provides a crucial guideline: time arbitrage is most effective when there is significant price volatility (high $p(t_1)/p(t_2)$ ratio), low task-specific delay sensitivity (small $\gamma_j$), and tasks have substantial resource demands or durations (large $d_j \cdot \tau_j$). These conditions are frequently met in cloud environments with spot instance pricing and batch workloads, leading to substantial savings.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (Deadline Satisfaction).} If all tasks have $\text{urgency} > \text{max\_deferral}$, no deadlines are violated.

\textbf{Proof.} By construction, tasks are forced to execute when their urgency (time to deadline) falls below a predefined threshold, $\text{max\_deferral}$. This $\text{max\_deferral}$ is not a fixed global value but is dynamically determined for each task based on its deferrability $\delta_k$ and its original deadline $dl_k$, ensuring that tasks are allocated before their deadlines are breached. Specifically, a task $j_k$ with deferrability $\delta_k$ can be delayed up to $(1 - \delta_k) \cdot (dl_k - a_k)$ from its arrival time $a_k$, where $dl_k - a_k$ is its total available time. The scheduler ensures that tasks are scheduled within this window. $\square$

\subsection{Discussion}

\textbf{Economic Impact.} For a \$10,000/month cloud deployment, our approach could yield substantial annual savings of \$111,360, demonstrating its significant economic value.

\textbf{Deployment Feasibility.} The proposed scheduler requires:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Task deferrability labels (can be inferred from task type or user-defined policies)
    \item Price predictions (can use historical averages or more sophisticated forecasting models)
    \item Queue management (a standard feature in most modern schedulers)
\end{itemize}
All these requirements are readily available or implementable within existing production systems, making our approach highly practical.

\textbf{Limitations.} Our current study has several limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic workload}: The evaluation uses synthetic workloads, which may not fully capture the complexities and variability of real-world cloud environments. Future work will involve validation with real-world traces from public datasets (e.g., Google Cluster Traces) or commercial cloud platforms to ensure broader applicability and robustness.
    \item \textbf{Simple price model}: The price model assumes a deterministic diurnal pattern. Real prices are often stochastic and influenced by various market factors. Incorporating more sophisticated, stochastic price models, potentially leveraging machine learning for price forecasting, is a direction for future research to enhance the realism and adaptability of our framework.
    \item \textbf{No migration overhead evaluation}: While migration costs are defined in our model, their impact was not explicitly evaluated in the current set of experiments. A detailed analysis of migration overhead and its trade-offs with cost savings, including scenarios with varying data sizes and migration frequencies, will be part of future work.
\end{itemize}
We plan to address these limitations in future work to further enhance the robustness and applicability of our time-arbitrage scheduling framework.

% Conclusion
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented the first time-arbitrage scheduler for heterogeneous cloud computing, inspired by power grid dispatch. By deliberately deferring non-urgent tasks to low-price periods, we achieve \textbf{27.45\% cost reduction} while maintaining \textbf{100\% task completion}.

\textbf{Key Contributions.} Our work makes the following significant contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Formal analogy}: We establish the first formal analogy between power grid dispatch and cloud scheduling, providing a novel conceptual framework for temporal resource optimization in cloud environments that highlights shared challenges and solution patterns.
    \item \textbf{Multi-level temporal hierarchy}: We introduce a multi-level temporal hierarchy (seconds to days) that allows for flexible and efficient scheduling decisions across different time scales, enabling fine-grained control over task deferral and resource allocation.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Future Work.} Building upon this foundation, our future research will focus on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Real-world deployment}: Exploring real-world deployment and validation on platforms such as Alibaba Cloud and DingTalk, addressing practical challenges like system integration, data privacy, and security, and demonstrating tangible benefits in production environments.
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement learning integration}: Integrating reinforcement learning for adaptive scheduling policies that can dynamically learn and adjust to evolving cloud environments and price patterns, offering enhanced adaptability and performance in highly dynamic and unpredictable scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Multi-region arbitrage}: Investigating multi-region arbitrage opportunities through geographic load shifting to further optimize costs and improve resilience.
    \item \textbf{Carbon-aware scheduling}: Developing carbon-aware scheduling strategies that align task execution with periods of high renewable energy availability, contributing to sustainable cloud computing.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Open Source.} Our simulator and datasets will be released at \url{https://github.com/openclaw/time-arbitrage}.
